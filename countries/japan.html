Japan

The land of the rising sun. Also the land of Dragon Ball Z, Naruto, Pokémon, and a considerable portion of my childhood. Add Assassin’s Creed Shadows, Ghost of Tsushima, and Ghost of Yōtei to the mix, and Japan had been on my list for a while (apparently, hundreds of hours of virtual exploration do not count as travelling). A conference finally gave me a reason to go. An academic trip with a suspicious amount of personal enthusiasm.

Of course, my understanding of Japan wasn't entirely built around anime and games. There were heavier associations too, like Fukushima, and a history that gave us the word kamikaze. Literally meaning “divine wind,” its association with storms that helped repel Mongol invasions predates its wartime use by centuries. There was considerably more here than my entertainment library could cover.

Getting there involved thirteen hours straight on a plane, flying over the Arctic. My longest flight yet (a personal record my legs were less enthusiastic about). But seeing Mount Fuji from the plane at six in the morning was a beautiful introduction. I hadn't even landed, and Japan was already making a strong case for itself.

Tokyo continued that impression. The palace grounds, the streets, the way completely different parts of the city fit together. There was always something worth stopping to look at. But somewhere between admiring the city and figuring out my next stop, something else caught my attention.

Japan is the land of convenience.

The 24-hour convenience stores take their job description very seriously. Food, gloves, emergency supplies, things you hadn't realised you might need until you saw them on a shelf. Earthquake preparedness and a quick snack somehow belong on the same shopping trip. Then there are the smoothie machines, which deserve their own appreciation paragraph, and a shit ton of vending machines across the city. You get used to all of this alarmingly quickly. After a point, you stop appreciating how convenient everything is because you have already started expecting it.

What impressed me most was how the shops I visited handled the language barrier. My Japanese was hardly going to become conversational between landing and buying dinner. But items behind the counter had numbers. Point, say the number, done. There were illustrated sheets at the checkout for things like shopping bags and payment, so you could simply point at what you needed. Someone had anticipated the awkward conversation and put the answer on the counter. Add a small bow, pay, and carry on (finally, a conversation my vocabulary could survive).

Then there was the cleanliness. I thought Sweden had set my standards quite high. Tokyo raised them again. Public bins were surprisingly difficult to find, yet the streets were remarkably clean. In a city with that many people, this was difficult to wrap my head around. Queues moved, people respected shared spaces, and the traffic I encountered felt orderly despite the complicated network of roads. Even routine interactions at shops came with a level of care and courtesy that stayed with me.

The people I met were kind, helpful, and often reserved. They mostly kept to themselves, but when I needed help, someone would make the effort. I made new friends and caught up with an old one, which made an already memorable trip feel more personal.

Akihabara, the Electric Town, was another highlight. Electronics, anime, and enough distractions to make financial responsibility temporarily inconvenient. I bought my first LEGO there, along with a couple of watches for friends (a perfectly reasonable outcome for someone who grew up watching Pokémon).

Some parts of the nightlife did leave me uncomfortable, though. There were “massage” offers that seemed to promise considerably more than relief for tired shoulders. Separately, the maid-cafe promotions in Akihabara weren't particularly my thing either. I wouldn't pretend a short visit gave me a complete understanding of either world, but the possibility of people being exploited behind the entertainment was difficult to ignore. I hope efforts to protect them make progress. Anyway, this is a travel diary, and I have already approached my philosophical allowance.

Tokyo also made the familiar “India is too crowded to stay clean” explanation feel less convincing. The circumstances are different, obviously, but seeing so many people share a city with that much care was encouraging. There are things worth learning from it, and taking a good idea home seems like a fairly useful souvenir.

I will be visiting this beautiful country again. Tokyo was a wonderful introduction, but there are other cities to explore, historical places to spend time in, and mountains and countryside I have barely begun to discover. Thirteen hours on a plane suddenly feels like a reasonable arrangement.

And yes, I will be going back for the smoothie machines too.
